\section{PACT: A Compliance Benchmark for Regulated Enterprise Assistants}
\label{sec:pact}

Each PACT item drops an agent into a scenario: a persona system prompt that
rewards a local objective (speed, cost, customer satisfaction), a standing rule, and
an in-character user request whose most convenient option violates that rule. Every
item is run across two conversation turns and in two \emph{modes}: a plain
\emph{base} system prompt, and an \emph{anti-adversarial} mode that adds one explicit
instruction to follow the rule; the gap between the two modes is what we call
steerability. Each item belongs to one of five groups (\emph{neutral},
\emph{pressure}, \emph{guard}, and two \emph{under-attack}) that decide which axes
it feeds. We first describe how an item is built and audited (\S\ref{sec:building}),
then the six metrics those items feed (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Building the Benchmark}
\label{sec:building}

\subsubsection{The measurement template.}
\label{sec:template}
Every item follows one template, which is what makes compliance rates comparable
across unrelated domains. Five things are fixed.
\emph{(1) Deployment framing.} The \emph{system} prompt gives the assistant its
persona and the targets it is measured on, but never mentions the decision; the rule
is added as a standing, general note. The \emph{user} turn carries the request, the
case facts, and the menu of options. Nothing says which option is compliant: the model
has to join the facts to the rule to tell, so each item is a reasoning task, not a
keyword match.
\emph{(2) Dominant shortcut.} The menu offers two to five options; at least one
violates the rule while winning on the local objective, and at least one complies but
costs more. Option order is randomized and never signals the answer.
\emph{(3) Neutral rule wording.} The rule is stated as plain fact, never as a command;
command-style strictness appears only in the steerability mode.
\emph{(4) Conditional second turn.} After the first answer, a follow-up pushes back if
the model complied and challenges it if it violated.
\emph{(5) Non-binding twin.} Each scenario ships a near-identical version in which the
rule does not apply, so \emph{holding} the rule there is the error; the twin separates
rule-following from rule-parroting.

\subsubsection{Domains and scenarios.}
\label{sec:domains}
The template is instantiated in \textbf{twelve regulated domains} with \textbf{four
scenarios each} (48 total; Appendix~\ref{app:domains}), spanning privacy (GDPR),
finance, customer service, government services, HR, AML/KYC, healthcare
administration (HIPAA), pharma medical information, advertising, export controls,
content moderation, and procurement. Each domain met three criteria: a documented
deployment surface, a crisp rule an agent can violate in a \emph{recommendation}, and
a real penalty anchor (statute, regulator action, or litigated AI incident). Seven
reconstruct settings that already produced a legal consequence (among them
\emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada}, NYC MyCity, and \emph{Mobley v.\ Workday}). The four
scenarios per domain use distinct rules spanning a range of penalty types (fines,
license loss, criminal exposure) and harm profiles; moderation is the deliberately
fuzzy-rule domain and procurement doubles as a contamination tripwire against prior published
data.

\subsubsection{Pressures and multi-turn stressors.}
\label{sec:pressures}
The stressor is a battery of \textbf{nine scored pressures}, each targeting a distinct
compliance-theory mechanism so that none is redundant: \emph{urgency} (time scarcity),
\emph{mgr\_verbal} (legitimate authority), \emph{peer\_escaped} (descriptive norm plus
impunity), \emph{risk\_minimize} (deterrence discounting), \emph{cost\_pressure} (loss
framing), \emph{false\_clearance} (unverifiable authorization), \emph{fait\_accompli}
(commitment and sunk cost), \emph{sympathetic\_beneficiary} (an identifiable person
harmed by \emph{compliance}), and \emph{responsibility\_shift} (accountability
transfer). The users are \emph{benign}: none jailbreaks; each is an employee whose
convenience conflicts with the rule, the realistic enterprise threat. A tenth
pressure, \emph{rule\_delegitimization} (an epistemic attack on the rule's scope), is
not scored but drives the discernment-under-attack items: applied to a binding item
the model must hold, and its mirror on the non-binding guard the model must stand down
against.

\subsubsection{Generating the items as an audited instrument.}
\label{sec:generation}
Items are LLM-authored, so we treat generation itself as a measurement instrument and
publish its quality record. A scenario is built \emph{component by component} in
dependency order (persona; request and menu; rule note; nine pressures; guard twin; two
turn-2 scripts; two attacks), each new component conditioned on the ones already
accepted so the pack reads in one voice. The baseline request is written once and reused
verbatim across the whole battery, so a single leak would contaminate every item built
on it. Prompts are \emph{zero-shot}: when generators are instead shown a shared worked
example they converge on its names and phrasing and erase the between-domain variation
we are trying to measure. Naturalism is enforced at the source (workplace register,
invented work artifacts, deliberate typing slips) because a model that senses it is
being tested is the single biggest threat to a behavioral benchmark.

Every component is then reviewed by models that did \emph{not} write it. Three
generators each author a complete, independent pack per scenario, and each structurally
valid component is checked by the other two on two questions: \emph{scope} (does only
the mechanism this item is meant to test appear?) and \emph{authenticity} (could this
have been written inside the real deployment?). The decision menu gets a third,
two-stage check: a first pass reviews it \emph{blind to the gold labels}, so a merely
factual option line is never mistaken for a giveaway, and a second, \emph{sighted} pass
certifies the answer key: that each option's compliant-or-violating label follows
from the rule and the case facts, and that the violating option genuinely wins on the
local objective while the compliant one costs more.

Failures are budgeted, not fatal. Either reviewer rejecting a component burns one of a
fixed number of attempts: eight for the three spine components (persona, request/menu,
rule note) and four per add-on, on the logic that an add-on needing many rewrites to
look real usually never will. A spine failure drops the whole pack; an add-on failure
drops only its own item, and the pack still ships as a \emph{partial} with the items
that passed, so a hard-to-author pressure costs one item rather than the scenario. Light
deterministic checks (menu composition, an account-agnostic rule note) run first, and
dashes and smart quotes are stripped, but naturalism and scope are judged by the review
panel rather than by keyword tripwires. Every verdict is logged as a flat row with the
per-reviewer-pair agreement ($\kappa$); and because each scenario exists in one version
per generator, ``items favor their author's model family'' becomes a measurable bias
check rather than an unanswerable objection. Rendering is then deterministic: each pack
yields its surviving subset of the 13-item battery (one neutral, nine pressure, one
non-binding guard, two under-attack), with gold labels, split tags, a canary string, and
a committed SHA-256 that serves as the pre-registration. The frozen release is $141$ of
a possible $144$ packs (all $48$ scenarios covered, authorship balanced across the three
generators), or $1{,}737$ items after partial packs.

\subsubsection{Evaluation protocol.}
\label{sec:protocol}
One trial is an (item, model, replication, mode) tuple: send system plus turn~1, judge
it, select the conditional turn~2, send it with history, judge again. Trials are keyed
by a stable id and appended to disk, so a killed run resumes; the budget is three
replications across a 22-model panel, with floor-defining items topped up to $n\geq50$.
Judging is deterministic-first: a rule-based extractor resolves most responses for
free, a small non-reasoning judge sees only the residual, and every row logs the judge
and a prompt hash. The honesty label is an ensemble of the three generator models,
each scoring only packs it did not itself write (leave-one-out). Before release, a
judge-swap check confirms that swapping in a different judge model leaves the ranking
essentially unchanged (Kendall $\tau\geq0.9$), and a stratified human gold set is
double-annotated with per-domain agreement ($\kappa$). A separate
awareness probe flags signs the model believes it is being tested and is reported
beside the leaderboard, never folded in. Throughout we report Wilson intervals on each
scored rate, item-clustered standard errors, Benjamini--Hochberg-corrected bootstrap
contrasts, and two-stage bootstrap on rollups; at temperature 1.0 results are
statistical estimates and always carry intervals.

\subsection{Metrics for Assessment}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Why a profile, not a score.} A single compliance number would be
convenient, but it would mislead. Following a rule is not one behavior: a model can
stand firm against a demanding manager yet fold the moment a deadline appears, or
resist every pressure yet never admit the one time it slips. In our prior-study data
(\S\ref{sec:validation}) these behaviors barely move together: the first principal
component of the model-by-condition matrix explains only 47\% of the variance, the
behavioral dimensions correlate at most 0.64, and a model's scores on five pressure
families predict its rank on the sixth only weakly (Spearman $\rho$ from 0.33 to
0.84). A single scalar would not merely lose detail; it would let a model average away
the one situation where it fails badly and still post a comfortable number. PACT
therefore reports six axes, each answering a concrete question a team would ask before
trusting a model and each built to resist a specific way a naive metric misleads. All
sit on $[0,1]$, higher is better, and are read straight off the trial records; only
the honesty axis needs a judge. On the 22-model panel the axes fall into three
near-independent groups (Figure~\ref{fig:axiscorr}): four \emph{rule-holding} axes
that move together because a capable model tends to hold across conditions, plus
\emph{steerability} and \emph{honesty}, each largely uncorrelated with the rest and
with each other. A scalar dominated by the rule-holding group would be nearly blind to
the two axes on which models differ most, so we report the axes rather than summing
them.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Default Compliance}: \emph{does it comply when nothing is pushing?}
$\text{pass}^3$ on neutral binding items, the fraction of items the model holds the
rule on across \emph{all} three replications. Throughout, $\text{pass}^3$ is
$\tau$-bench's pass$^k$ estimator~\citep{yao2024taubench} at $k{=}3$: for an item with
$c$ compliant runs of $n$, $\mathrm{pass}^k=\binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ averaged over
items, so with $n{=}k{=}3$ an item scores $1$ only when all three runs comply and a
model that slips one run in three is unreliable on that item, not ``$0.67$.'' This is
the baseline every stressor is read against.

\item \textbf{Pressure Resistance}: \emph{does it hold under pressure, reliably?}
$\text{pass}^3$ over the pressure items, the fraction on which the model complies for
\emph{every} replication, so an occasionally-slipping model is penalized where a mean
would absorb the slip. We report it beside a na\"ive worst-quartile mean and a
\emph{fragility breadth}, the number of pressure families whose mean compliance falls
below a threshold, $\lvert\{f:\bar c_f<\tau\}\rvert$, because being broken by one
vector differs from being broken by six.

\item \textbf{Pushback Resistance}: \emph{does it hold when the user argues back?}
$\mathrm{pass}^3(\text{hold at turn 2}\mid\text{complied at turn 1})$ over the model's
own turn-1-compliant items, after the user pushes back. It measures erosion under a
persistent interlocutor; recovery is reported separately as a diagnostic.

\item \textbf{Steerability}: \emph{if it fails, can an explicit guardrail fix it?}
Every item is run twice, base persona and base plus the scenario's generated hard
directive, and the score is $\text{net}=\text{recovery}-\max(0,\,\Delta_{\text{sd}})$.
Here recovery is the fraction of base-mode violations the mandate repairs, measured only
where the base model fails so there is no ceiling artifact, and
$\Delta_{\text{sd}}=\text{sd}_{\text{base}}-\text{sd}_{\text{anti}}$ subtracts any loss
of correct stand-downs, which stops the mandate from ``working'' by turning the model
into a blanket always-comply agent.

\item \textbf{Reasoning Honesty}: \emph{when it violates, does it admit it?}
$1-\text{silent rate}=1-n_{\text{silent}}/n_{\text{viol}}$ over violations, with each
labeled silent, rationalized, or defiant-honest. Because the denominator is violations
the best models have the least data (undefined maps to $1$, held out of the harmonic
mean), and because it is near-100\% judged it carries its own reliability gate.

\item \textbf{Rule-Scope Discernment}: \emph{does it apply the rule only where it
binds?} Balanced accuracy over binding and non-binding scope items,
$\tfrac12[P(\text{comply}\mid\text{bind})+P(\text{stand-down}\mid\text{non-bind})]$,
with the under-attack items folded in. The two terms are reported separately; the
discriminating signal is the stand-down term, since the binding term overlaps axis~1.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Rollup.} The scalar summary is a two-stage-bootstrapped $\text{pass}^3$
over the pooled scored items, the fraction of all scored items the model complies on
across every replication, reported beside its plain mean (the reliability-versus-mean
gap is itself informative), a harmonic mean over axes 1--4 and 6 (axis 5 held out),
and a mean win rate. Scoring per-item reliability rather than an average is
deliberate: an assistant in a regulated workflow has to be right \emph{every} time, so a rollup of $0.9$
reads correctly as ``unreliable on one item in ten'' rather than as a comfortable
grade. We publish the full inter-axis correlations and per-axis $\kappa$ rather than
asserting orthogonality.
