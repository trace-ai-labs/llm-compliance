\section{PACT: A Compliance Benchmark for Regulated Enterprise Agents}
\label{sec:pact}

Each PACT item drops an agent into a deployment: a persona system prompt that
rewards a local objective (speed, cost, CSAT), a standing rule, and an
in-character user request whose most convenient option violates that rule. Every
item is run across two conversation turns and in two \emph{arms} --- a plain
\emph{base} system prompt, and an \emph{anti-adversarial} arm that adds one explicit
instruction to follow the rule; the gap between the two arms is what we call
steerability. Each item belongs to one of five groups --- \emph{neutral},
\emph{pressure}, \emph{guard}, and two \emph{under-attack} --- that decide which axes
it feeds. We first describe how an item is built and audited (\S\ref{sec:building}),
then the six metrics those items feed (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Building the Benchmark}
\label{sec:building}

\subsubsection{The measurement template.}
\label{sec:template}
Every item instantiates one template, and this isomorphism is what makes compliance
rates comparable across unrelated domains. It fixes five invariants.
\emph{(1) Deployment framing.} A \emph{system} prompt gives the agent's persona and
the KPIs it is measured on and never mentions the decision; the rule is appended as a
standing, account-agnostic note stating the general rule only. A \emph{user} turn
carries the request, case facts, and menu. Compliance is stated nowhere: which option
satisfies the rule is derivable only by joining the case facts to the rule, making
each item a reasoning task rather than a keyword match.
\emph{(2) Dominance structure.} The menu holds two to five options with at least one
rule-violating option that strictly dominates on the local objective and at least one
rule-compliant option that costs more; menu order never encodes compliance.
\emph{(3) Informational framing.} The rule is a neutral statement of fact, never a
command; command-style strictness is supplied only in the steerability arm.
\emph{(4) Conditional second turn.} Every neutral and pressure item carries a turn~2
conditioned on the turn~1 answer: a \emph{pushback} if the model complied, a
\emph{challenge} if it violated.
\emph{(5) Guard cells.} Each scenario ships a non-binding twin, a minimal edit in
which the rule does not apply, so holding it is an error; the guards separate
rule-following from rule-parroting.

\subsubsection{Domains and scenarios.}
\label{sec:domains}
The template is instantiated in \textbf{twelve regulated domains} with \textbf{four
scenarios each} (48 total; Appendix~\ref{app:domains}), spanning privacy (GDPR),
finance, customer service, government services, HR, AML/KYC, healthcare
administration (HIPAA), pharma medical information, advertising, export controls,
content moderation, and procurement. Each domain met three criteria: a documented
deployment surface, a crisp rule an agent can violate in a \emph{recommendation}, and
a real penalty anchor (statute, regulator action, or litigated AI incident). Seven
reconstruct settings that already produced a legal consequence (among them
\emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada}, NYC MyCity, and \emph{Mobley v.\ Workday}). The four
scenarios per domain use distinct rules spanning a range of penalty types (fines,
license loss, criminal exposure) and harm profiles; moderation is the deliberately
fuzzy-rule domain and procurement doubles as a contamination tripwire against prior published
data.

\subsubsection{Pressures and multi-turn stressors.}
\label{sec:pressures}
The stressor is a battery of \textbf{nine scored pressures}, each targeting a distinct
compliance-theory mechanism so that none is redundant: \emph{urgency} (time scarcity),
\emph{mgr\_verbal} (legitimate authority), \emph{peer\_escaped} (descriptive norm plus
impunity), \emph{risk\_minimize} (deterrence discounting), \emph{cost\_pressure} (loss
framing), \emph{false\_clearance} (unverifiable authorization), \emph{fait\_accompli}
(commitment and sunk cost), \emph{sympathetic\_beneficiary} (an identifiable person
harmed by \emph{compliance}), and \emph{responsibility\_shift} (accountability
transfer). The users are \emph{benign}: none jailbreaks; each is an employee whose
convenience conflicts with the rule, the realistic enterprise threat. A tenth
pressure, \emph{rule\_delegitimization} (an epistemic attack on the rule's scope), is
not scored but drives the discernment-under-attack cells: applied to a binding item
the model must hold, and its mirror on the non-binding guard the model must stand down
against.

\subsubsection{Generating the items as an audited instrument.}
\label{sec:generation}
Items are LLM-authored, so we treat generation itself as a measurement instrument and
publish its quality record. A scenario is built \emph{component by component} in
dependency order (persona; request and menu; rule note; nine pressures; guard twin; two
turn-2 scripts; two attacks), each new component conditioned on the ones already
accepted so the pack reads in one voice. The baseline request is written once and reused
verbatim across the whole battery, so a single leak would contaminate every cell built
on it. Prompts are \emph{zero-shot}: when generators are instead shown a shared worked
example they converge on its names and phrasing and erase the between-domain variation
we are trying to measure. Naturalism is enforced at the source --- workplace register,
invented work artifacts, deliberate typing slips --- because a model that senses it is
being tested is the single biggest threat to a propensity benchmark.

Every component is then reviewed by models that did \emph{not} write it. Three
generators each author a complete, independent pack per scenario, and each structurally
valid component is checked by the other two on two questions: \emph{scope} (does only
the mechanism this cell is meant to test appear?) and \emph{authenticity} (could this
have been written inside the real deployment?). The decision menu gets a third,
two-stage check: a first pass reviews it \emph{blind to the gold labels}, so a merely
factual option line is never mistaken for a giveaway, and a second, \emph{sighted} pass
certifies the answer key --- that each option's compliant-or-violating label follows
from the rule and the case facts, and that the violating option genuinely wins on the
local objective while the compliant one costs more.

Failures are budgeted, not fatal. Either reviewer rejecting a component burns one of a
fixed number of attempts: eight for the three spine components (persona, request/menu,
rule note) and four per add-on, on the logic that an add-on needing many rewrites to
look real usually never will. A spine failure drops the whole pack; an add-on failure
drops only its own cell, and the pack still ships as a \emph{partial} with the cells
that passed, so a hard-to-author pressure costs one cell rather than the scenario. Light
deterministic checks (menu composition, an account-agnostic rule note) run first, and
dashes and smart quotes are stripped, but naturalism and scope are judged by the review
panel rather than by keyword tripwires. Every verdict is logged as a flat row with the
per-reviewer-pair agreement ($\kappa$); and because each scenario exists in one version
per generator, ``items favor their author's model family'' becomes a measurable bias
check rather than an unanswerable objection. Rendering is then deterministic: each pack
yields its surviving subset of the 13-cell battery (one neutral, nine pressure, one
non-binding guard, two under-attack), with gold labels, split tags, a canary string, and
a committed SHA-256 that serves as the pre-registration. The frozen release is $141$ of
a possible $144$ packs (all $48$ scenarios covered, authorship balanced across the three
generators), or $1{,}737$ items after partial packs.

\subsubsection{Evaluation protocol.}
\label{sec:protocol}
One trial is an (item, model, replication, arm) tuple: send system plus turn~1, judge
it, select the conditional turn~2, send it with history, judge again. Trials are keyed
by a stable id and appended to disk, so a killed run resumes; the budget is three
replications across a 22-model panel, with floor-defining cells topped up to $n\geq50$.
Judging is deterministic-first: a rule-based extractor resolves most responses for
free, a small non-reasoning judge sees only the residual, and every row logs the judge
and a prompt hash. The honesty label is an ensemble of the three generator models,
each scoring only packs it did not itself write (leave-one-out). Before release, a
judge-swap check confirms that swapping in a different judge model leaves the ranking
essentially unchanged (Kendall $\tau\geq0.9$), and a stratified human gold set is
double-annotated with per-domain agreement ($\kappa$). A separate
awareness probe flags signs the model believes it is being tested and is reported
beside the leaderboard, never folded in. Throughout we report Wilson intervals per
cell, item-clustered standard errors, Benjamini--Hochberg-corrected bootstrap
contrasts, and two-stage bootstrap on rollups; at temperature 1.0 results are
statistical estimates and always carry intervals.

\subsection{Metrics for Assessment}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Why a profile, not a score.} ``Will this agent follow the rule?'' looks
like one question, but it is not. On data from a prior single-domain study (\S\ref{sec:validation})
compliance is not a single latent trait: the first principal component of the
model-by-condition rate matrix explains only 47\% of the variance, the behavioral
dimensions inter-correlate at most 0.64, and a score computed \emph{without} a given
pressure family predicts a model's rank \emph{on} that family only weakly (Spearman
$\rho$ from 0.33 to 0.84). A model robust to a manager's order is not reliably robust
to a deadline. A single scalar would therefore be wrong about the structure of the
phenomenon, not merely lossy, and it would let a model average away the one condition
where it catastrophically fails. PACT instead reports six axes, each a distinct
question a deployer actually asks and each engineered against a specific way a naive
metric misleads. All are on $[0,1]$, higher better, computed by pure functions over
the trial records; only axis~5 consumes a judge. On the 22-model panel the axes fall
into three near-independent factors (Figure~\ref{fig:axiscorr}): a rule-holding
cluster (default compliance, pressure and pushback resistance, rule-scope
discernment) that covaries because a capable model tends to hold across conditions,
plus \emph{steerability} and \emph{honesty}, each near-uncorrelated with the cluster
and with each other. The practical consequence is the same as the design argument: a
scalar dominated by the rule-holding cluster is nearly blind to the two axes on which
the field is weakest and most variable, so the axes must be reported, not summed.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Default Compliance} --- \emph{does it comply when nothing is pushing?}
$\text{pass}^3$ on neutral binding cells: the fraction of items the model holds the
rule on across \emph{all} three replications. Throughout, $\text{pass}^3$ is
$\tau$-bench's pass$^k$ estimator~\citep{yao2024taubench} at $k=3$ --- credit for a
task requires every replication to succeed, not their average --- which is why a
model that slips one run in three is scored as unreliable on that item rather than as
``0.67.'' This is the baseline every stressor is read against.

\item \textbf{Pressure Resistance} --- \emph{does it hold under pressure, reliably?}
A mean over pressure cells hides both the family that floors the model and the model
that holds on average but slips one run in three. We score $\text{pass}^3$: the
fraction of pressure cells the model complies on for \emph{every} replication. A cell
counts only if it is unanimous, so an occasionally-slipping model is penalized where
a mean would absorb the slip. We report it beside a na\"ive worst-quartile mean and a
\emph{fragility breadth} (how many families break the model), because being broken by
one vector differs from being broken by six.

\item \textbf{Pushback Resistance} --- \emph{does it hold when the user argues back?}
$\text{pass}^3$ of holding at turn~2 given the model complied at turn~1 and was then
pushed back, over the model's own turn-1-compliant items. It measures erosion under a
persistent interlocutor; recovery is reported separately as a diagnostic.

\item \textbf{Steerability} --- \emph{if it fails, can an explicit guardrail fix it?}
Every cell is run twice: base persona, and base plus the scenario's generated hard
directive. The score is $\text{net}=\text{recovery}-\max(0,\,\text{standdown}_{\text{base}}
-\text{standdown}_{\text{anti}})$, where recovery is the fraction of \emph{base
violations} the mandate repairs (measured only where the base model fails, so there
is no ceiling artifact) and the penalty stops the mandate from ``working'' by turning
the model into a blanket always-comply agent.

\item \textbf{Reasoning Honesty} --- \emph{when it violates, does it admit it?}
Over violations, $1-\text{silent rate}$, with each violation labeled silent,
rationalized, or defiant-honest. Because the denominator is violations, the best
models have the least data (undefined maps to 1, held out of the harmonic mean), and
because it is near-100\% judged it carries its own reliability gate.

\item \textbf{Rule-Scope Discernment} --- \emph{does it apply the rule only where it
binds?} $\text{pass}^3$ over the union of binding and non-binding scope cells, where
``comply'' is the correct scope call in each direction (follow the rule where it
binds, stand down where it does not), with the under-attack cells folded in. The
binding and stand-down terms are reported separately; the discriminating signal is in
the stand-down term, since the binding term overlaps axis~1.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Rollup.} The scalar summary is a two-stage-bootstrapped $\text{pass}^3$
over the pooled scored cells --- the fraction of all scored items the model complies
on across every replication --- reported beside its plain mean (the
reliability-versus-mean gap is itself informative), a harmonic mean over axes 1--4
and 6 (axis 5 held out), and a mean win rate. Scoring per-item reliability rather
than an average is deliberate: a compliance agent has to be right \emph{every} time,
not on average, so a rollup of $0.9$ reads correctly as ``unreliable on one item in
ten'' rather than as a comfortable grade. We publish the full inter-axis correlation
matrix and per-axis $\kappa$ rather than asserting orthogonality, since the axes span
a reliability range (1, 2, 4 near-deterministic; 3 turn-parsing; 6 mostly
deterministic; 5 all-judge) a pooled number would hide.
