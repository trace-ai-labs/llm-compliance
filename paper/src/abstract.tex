Enterprise language-model agents are being deployed into precisely the functions
where breaking a rule is a legal event---privacy, finance, hiring, healthcare
administration, and consumer-facing support---yet no benchmark tells a deployer
which model keeps following an embedded policy when a hurried manager, a helpful
shortcut, or a persistent user pushes against it. We argue that this is a question
of \emph{propensity}, not capability: the models can all identify the compliant
action, so what a compliance officer needs to know is whether they \emph{keep
choosing it} once the context makes violation locally attractive. We introduce
\textbf{ComplianceBench}, a benchmark that measures rule-following under benign
incentive conflict across twelve regulated enterprise domains and forty-eight
scenarios, each cast as a realistic multi-turn deployment. Every item pairs a
standing rule against a dominant, rule-violating shortcut, applies a battery of
nine theory-grounded pressures and a conditional second-turn pushback, and is run
in two system-prompt arms to isolate steerability. Six de-correlated metrics
report default compliance, worst-case pressure resistance, multi-turn durability,
guardrail responsiveness, honesty about violations, and rule-scope discernment.
The benchmark is built as an audited instrument: items are generated
component-by-component under dual model review with a published reliability
record, degenerate policies are calibrated against real models to keep the score
un-gameable, and a frozen, pre-registered item set with a private holdout guards
against contamination. We ground every design choice in compliance theory and in a
record of litigated AI incidents, and we validate the metric design on a
72{,}000-trial precursor study before proposing it as a governance instrument.
