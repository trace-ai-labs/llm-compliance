\section{Discussion and Social Impact}
\label{sec:discussion}

ComplianceBench is aimed at a concrete governance decision. A bank, hospital, or
municipal IT office choosing an agent for a regulated workflow currently has no
instrument that answers ``which model holds this rule under a rushed manager?'' The
nearest available signals, aggregate knowledge scores and refusal rates on malicious
prompts, are silent on it, and the field's own evidence says single-turn numbers
overstate the answer. By reporting a per-domain, floor-sensitive, multi-turn
propensity profile, the benchmark gives a deployer something they can act on: filter
the leaderboard to their own domain, read the worst-quartile floor rather than the
flattering mean, check whether an explicit guardrail actually moves their candidate
model (steerability), and see whether the model that violates at least admits it
(honesty). Two of the design's findings are already interventions a deployer can
apply today. Rephrasing a rule imperatively rather than informationally sharply
raises compliance, and removing quantitative penalty language from system prompts
avoids the ``fine is a price'' paradox for the models prone to it.

The instrument is also built for the regulator's and researcher's timescale. The
frozen item set, with its pre-registered hash, private holdout, and canary, is a
contamination hedge that lets the same benchmark be rerun on each new model
\emph{version}. No one currently tracks whether a vendor's silent update moved its
urgency floor, and a benchmark that catches that once becomes monitoring
infrastructure. Because the release is per-trial and every alternative aggregator
ships, the headline score is a recommendation with an argument rather than a
monopoly: a deployer with a different cost model for violations versus escalations
can re-rank under it.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} We name the ones reviewers check first.
\emph{Evaluation awareness}: a model that behaves differently when it detects
testing would undermine any propensity benchmark, so our mitigations are naturalism
enforced at generation and a dedicated awareness probe reported beside the score,
which at least measures the threat rather than assuming it away. \emph{Stated versus
enacted compliance}: recommendation-level compliance is an upper bound on what a
tool-using agent would actuate, and closing that gap is the explicit subject of a
follow-on actuated variant. The counter-objection is weaker than it looks, because
\emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada} established that a chatbot's \emph{statement} is itself
a liability surface~\citep{moffatt2024aircanada}. \emph{Judge circularity}: LLMs
judging LLMs on rule-following invites correlated blind spots, which the
deterministic first tier (covering roughly four-fifths of outcomes), the human gold
set, and the judge-swap $\tau$-gate defend against in three layers.
\emph{Generated items}: synthetic items could drift from deployment reality or favor
their author's model family, which the dual-guard review, structural validators,
human pack review, and the computable generator-versus-evaluatee interaction test
address directly. \emph{Panel size}: model-level factor analyses are indicative at a
12-model panel, so the item-level statistics over thousands of trials are the
load-bearing ones, and every newly scored model strengthens the model-level claims a
one-shot paper cannot.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

ComplianceBench measures whether enterprise LLM agents keep following an embedded
rule when benign incentive conflict, institutional pressure, and multi-turn pushback
make violation attractive. Its contribution is as much methodological as empirical:
a measurement template that makes compliance comparable across twelve regulated
domains, a theory-grounded pressure battery, a two-arm multi-turn protocol yielding
six de-correlated axes, an item-generation pipeline engineered to be auditable and
contamination-resistant, and a metric suite validated on a 72{,}000-trial precursor
and calibrated to resist degenerate policies. The failure class it measures has
already produced tribunal damages, a certified class action, and regulator orders,
and the instrument is designed to be rerun cheaply as models change. We release the
frozen item set, the per-trial outcomes, the generation and reviewer logs, and every
aggregator so that the benchmark can be re-analyzed, extended to new domains through
its promotion gate, and used as what regulated deployers currently lack: a standard
way to ask an agent, before trusting it with a rule, whether it will keep the rule
when keeping it costs something.

\section*{Ethical Statement}
This work is a defensive evaluation. It measures whether AI agents follow
regulations and enterprise policies, with the goal of helping deployers and
regulators identify models that fail to. All scenarios are synthetic
reconstructions; no real personal data, customer records, or confidential business
information is used, and the litigated incidents referenced are matters of public
record. A benchmark that characterizes how models can be pushed into non-compliance
could in principle inform an actor seeking to elicit that behavior, but the
pressures we catalog are ordinary workplace situations already ubiquitous in
deployment, not novel attack techniques, and the social value of letting deployers
detect and avoid non-compliant agents before they are trusted with regulated
decisions substantially outweighs this risk. We release the benchmark with a private
holdout to preserve its integrity as a governance instrument.
