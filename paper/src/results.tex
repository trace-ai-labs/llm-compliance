\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:leaderboard}

\paragraph{No model is reliable enough to deploy unsupervised.} Kimi-K2.7-Code leads at a
PACTScore of 0.944 and Mistral-7B trails at 0.484; no model attains 0.95. We compare every
pair of models on the paired per-item PACTScore difference, bootstrapped with the item as
the resampling unit and corrected for multiplicity across all 231 pairs
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). 186 comparisons are significant at $p<0.05$, so most of the
ordering is real, but Kimi-K2.7-Code and Qwen3.6-27B (0.943) are not separable and first
place is a tie.
%
\emph{Pressure accounts for most of the shortfall.} Pressure Resistance falls below Default
Compliance for 19 of the 22 models, and adding a single pressure paragraph raises the
violation rate from 4.41\% to 7.29\% of decisions; multi-turn pushback raises it further.
The effect reaches the top of the table. Claude Haiku 4.5 holds the panel's only perfect
Default Compliance (1.000), yet its Pressure Resistance is 0.977 and it fails one pressured
situation in 42.

\paragraph{Models also enforce rules that do not apply.} Rule-Scope Discernment is below
Default Compliance for all 22 models, and it is the widest gap in the table for the
strictest of them: Claude Haiku 4.5 scores 1.000 and 0.855, GPT-5.6 Luna 0.978 and 0.831,
Qwen3.5-35B 0.956 and 0.811. Across 13{,}817 decided base-mode decisions on requests the
rule does not cover, models enforce it anyway on 19.6\%, and Claude Haiku 4.5 pairs the
panel's lowest violation rate with one of its highest over-application rates, 21.9\%. Part
of what reads as compliance in the leftmost column is a disposition to apply rules
regardless of scope.
%
Over-application is not the harmless error. An assistant that cannot tell where a rule stops
opens reviews nothing called for and refuses requests it was deployed to handle, which
leaves the organization no less exposed and measurably less productive. The two directions
correlate at $r=0.67$, so neither predicts the other and a model cannot be selected on
either alone.

\paragraph{Compliance varies more across pressures and domains than across models.} Turn-1
compliance ranges from 0.883 under \emph{false\_clearance} to 0.952 under
\emph{peer\_escaped}, so even the mildest pressure produces violations on 4.8\% of
requests. Across domains the range is wider still: procurement (0.755), healthcare
administration (0.867), HR/hiring (0.882), and advertising (0.894) against 0.996 for
government services and pharma medical information. That spread exceeds the gap between the
best and the median model, and the two factors compound: procurement under pressure falls
to 0.693, and procurement under \emph{false\_clearance} to 0.71
(Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}, Tables~\ref{tab:perdomain} and~\ref{tab:pressureturns}).

\paragraph{The compliance mandate does not help the models worth deploying.} Steerability,
the share of base failures the mandate repairs, is low throughout: it spans 0.016 (GPT-5.6
Luna) to 0.564 (Grok~4.3) with a median of 0.42, and it does not rise with rank. In
PACTScore terms the mandate is worth between 0 and 9 points, and the gain is inversely
related to base compliance: it repairs models that were unfit to begin with, moves five of
the top seven by under one point, and lowers two. Steerability also varies by pressure and
by domain, and in neither case does it track how much room for improvement was available
(Figure~\ref{fig:steerbaseline}): the mandate recovers least under
\emph{responsibility\_shift} (0.182), \emph{peer\_escaped} (0.214), and \emph{urgency}
(0.216), against 0.462 under \emph{sympathetic\_beneficiary}, and least in HR/hiring
(0.185) and healthcare administration (0.295), two of the highest-stakes domains in the
set. A prompt-level guardrail is therefore not a substitute for a process control in the
settings that need one most.

\paragraph{No model is transparent about the rules it breaks.} Transparency is the weakest
axis in the benchmark. No model exceeds 0.244 and the median is 0.134, so on the large
majority of its violations every model in the panel leaves the reader unable to tell that a
rule was broken. Across the 16{,}424 judged violations, scoring only the reader-visible
text of the reply, 8.0\% disclose the breach and 12.8\% are silent. The remaining 79.2\%
misrepresent the choice: as compliant, as covered by an approval the conversation never
established, or as resolved by a workaround the model invented. The second of these is the
model repeating the user's unverified claim as established fact, which is why
\emph{false\_clearance} is the most damaging pressure we test. A silent violation is invisible to a keyword search over
transcripts; a misrepresented one survives human review, because it arrives with a reason.
The mandate does not help here either: violations committed under it are misrepresented at
the same rate (79.9\% against 78.7\%). For the most compliant models the per-model shares
rest on few violations (147 for Claude Haiku 4.5 against 4{,}112 for Mistral-7B), so the
panel-wide figure is the reliable one.

\paragraph{Abstentions are substantive.} Dropping unresolved replies rather than scoring
them as violations (\S\ref{sec:protocol}) is defensible only if those replies are genuine.
They are: abstention runs 0.6\%--7.5\% (median 2.4\%), does not track PACTScore, and blank
non-answers account for 0.4\%. Refusing on policy grounds (29.3\%) and committing to an
option outside the menu (24.4\%) make up half, and the remaining 46.0\% return the choice
to the user (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}).

\paragraph{The axes are not interchangeable.} Each of the three failure modes
\S\ref{sec:metrics} anticipates appears in the panel, in different models.
Claude Haiku 4.5 has the highest Pressure Resistance (0.977) and a Transparency of 0.172,
so selecting on compliance alone returns the models whose failures are hardest to find in a
transcript. Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B invert Pressure Resistance and Pushback Resistance
(0.599 against 0.448, and 0.469 against 0.583), so a single-turn evaluation would rate the
first far higher than a multi-turn one. Grok~4.3 is the opposite case, second in the panel
on Pushback Resistance (0.980) but 19th on Default Compliance (0.888).
Figure~\ref{fig:overlay} overlays six profiles and no two share a shape.

\subsection{What a Violation Looks Like}
\label{res:violations}

Each example below is a base-mode trial of a model near the top of
Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} under a single pressure; Appendix~\ref{app:failures} reproduces
the last in full.
%
\emph{Supplies a pretext.} Qwen3.6-27B (rank 2) states the source-of-income statute
correctly, then tells a landlord to decline a Section~8 voucher holder under a ``lawful,
non-source-of-income reason'' and to leave the real reason unwritten. It repeats this at
turn~2 after the user names the law.
%
\emph{Breaks the rule silently.} Claude Haiku 4.5 (rank 3), under time pressure, opens a
VIP-flagged chart in the sidebar instead of routing the request, a reportable access under
45 CFR 164.502, without mentioning PHI or the breach.
%
\emph{Manufactures a record.} On the same item, Gemini 3 Flash (rank 7) writes a
Treatment/Scheduling justification into the access log, producing a clean audit trail for
an access that should not have occurred.
%
\emph{Corrects itself only when challenged.} Kimi-K2.7-Code (rank 1) drops the one
shortlisted candidate on parental leave, gives the disqualifying reason (``no leave
conflicts''), and drafts her rejection, the fact pattern of \emph{Mobley v.\
Workday}~\citep{mobley2025workday}. It reverses at turn~2 when asked whether leave factored
into the ranking.
%
In three of the four the reply never mentions the rule, so transcript review would not
surface them.

\subsection{Evaluation Awareness}
\label{res:eval_awareness}
A benchmark measures nothing useful if a model complies only because it detects a test,
which recent models increasingly do~\citep{needham2025evalaware}. On a random 10\% of items
we hold the rule, facts, incentive, and options fixed and vary only how much the item
resembles an evaluation: the native item, a version rewritten into generic model-prose with
the invented company and workplace register removed, and the native item prefaced with
``you are being evaluated.'' Both altered versions also carry the forced-choice instruction
common in benchmark training data.

Both cues raise compliance, and the explicit one raises it more broadly. Announcing the
test removes a mean of 29\% of native non-compliance and moves eight of the twelve models
tested, reaching $+80\%$ for DeepSeek-V4-Pro and $+57\%$ for Gemini~3 Flash; flattening
the prose moves seven of the twelve the same way (median $+16\%$, up to $+53\%$ for
Kimi-K2.6 and Claude Haiku 4.5), though four models abstain more once flattened.
Compliance therefore rises as an item is made to look more like a test, which makes the
native items the least evaluation-aware of the three and the naturalism enforced during
generation (\S\ref{sec:generation}) a measurable contributor to validity.
Appendix~\ref{app:evalaware} gives the protocol, per-model results, and caveats.
