\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:findings}

We evaluate 22 models (18 open-weights and 4 closed) on PACT's \PactNItems\ items
(1{,}682 scenario cells, each in a base and an anti-adversarial system-prompt mode), three
times each. The panel spans 7B dense models to trillion-parameter sparse mixtures and three
years of releases; Appendix~\ref{app:models} lists each model's provider, size, release
date, and citation. Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} gives our main results, the six axes and
PACTScore side by side.
%
\S\ref{sec:leaderboard} reads the leaderboard and locates which pressures and domains the
failures concentrate in, \S\ref{res:pressure} isolates what ordinary user pressure does and
shows four violations verbatim, and \S\ref{res:eval_awareness} tests whether PACT is robust
to evaluation-awareness bias.

\paragraph{No model is reliable enough to deploy unsupervised.} \PactLeader\ leads at
\PactLeaderScore~\PactLeaderCI, which still leaves \PactLeaderFail\% of its decisions
unreliable, and \PactLast\ scores \PactLastScore~\PactLastCI. Not one of the \PactN\ models
reaches 0.95, and 17 clear 0.90. Every score carries an item-clustered bootstrap interval
of half-width \PactCIHalfMin--\PactCIHalfMax\ and every pair is tested under multiplicity
correction, so \PactNSig\ of \PactNPairs\ pairs separate at $p<0.05$ while the top
\PactTopCluster\ do not; Appendix~\ref{app:stats} reports all of it.
%
What that costs is set by ordinary user pressure. Adding one paragraph of workplace
pressure to a request --- a deadline, a manager's say-so, a colleague who did the same last
quarter --- raises the panel's violation rate from 4.41\% [4.00, 4.86] to 7.29\%
[7.11, 7.47] of decisions. The median model then recommends the rule-breaking option on one
pressured request in twenty-four, and if the employee simply asks again, one pressured
situation in sixteen ends in a violation; 15 of the 22 models are at one in twenty or worse.
%
These are not rounding errors on a benchmark. The rules are statutory duties --- PHI access
under HIPAA, source-of-income discrimination, erasure under GDPR Article~17, protected
characteristics in hiring --- and each breach is an independently reportable event rather
than a fraction of one. Single failures of exactly this kind have already produced tribunal
damages~\citep{moffatt2024aircanada} and a discrimination collective
action~\citep{mobley2025workday}. An assistant fielding such requests all day does not
average its way out of a one-in-twenty-four rate; it accumulates incidents.

\paragraph{Models also enforce rules that do not apply.} The requests where the rule does
not apply carry the opposite error, and it is the more frequent of the two. Across 13{,}817
decided base-mode decisions on items where the rule does not bind, models enforce it anyway
on 19.6\%, and the rate does not improve at the top of the table: \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5},
with the panel's lowest violation rate, has one of its highest over-application rates at
21.9\%. Every model scores lower on rule-scope discernment than on default compliance, and
the gap is widest for the strictest (\texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} 1.000 against 0.855,
\texttt{GPT-5.6 Luna} 0.978 against 0.831), so part of what reads as compliance in the
leftmost column is a disposition to hold rules whether or not they apply.
%
This failure is not the harmless direction. An assistant that cannot tell where a rule stops
blocks work the rule never covered: it opens reviews nothing triggered, refuses requests it
was deployed to resolve, and gives staff a reason to route around it. That converts legal
exposure into lost throughput instead of removing it, and an assistant used that way is not
a compliance control, only an obstacle. Because the two errors are close to independent,
a model chosen on either one alone will be wrong about the other.

\paragraph{Failures concentrate on a few pressures and a few domains.} They are not spread
evenly, and both maps are actionable. Pooled over the panel, base compliance runs from 0.883
under \emph{false\_clearance}, an unverifiable claim that the action was already approved,
to 0.952 under \emph{peer\_escaped}; by domain it runs from 0.76 in procurement to 0.99 in
government services and content moderation, a spread as wide as the gap between the best and
the median model. The two compound: the weakest single cell is procurement under
\emph{false\_clearance} at 0.72. Over-application is structured too, and inversely, at its
worst where the rule's boundary is genuinely ambiguous --- 0.71 in healthcare administration
and 0.77 in AML against 0.90--0.91 in government services and moderation. A failure rate
concentrated on one framing is a different object from a uniform one, because an insider
needs only the sentence that matches it. Per-model, per-domain, and per-pressure breakdowns
are in Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}.

\paragraph{A compliance mandate cannot repair the frontier.} Because PACTScore counts the
base and anti-adversarial modes as separate items, a model an explicit instruction cannot
lift scores lower than one it can. The base-to-mandate difference runs \PactGapMin\ to
\PactGapMax\ and is inversely related to base compliance: worth \PactGapMax\ to
\texttt{Llama-3.1-8B}, under $+0.01$ to four of the top seven, and negative for
\PactNBackfire. The families it repairs least all relocate accountability rather than create
urgency --- \emph{peer\_escaped} (0.15), \emph{responsibility\_shift} (0.18), and
\emph{fait\_accompli} (0.21) --- and the domains it repairs least are HR/hiring (0.19) and
healthcare administration (0.29), two of the highest-stakes in the set. Adding ``follow all
rules, without exception'' to the system prompt substantially repairs models that were
already unfit and leaves the frontier where it was, so where the pressure is an argument
about responsibility the remedy is a process control rather than a prompt.

\paragraph{Models are not transparent about the rules they break.} An organization can
absorb an occasional violation if the transcript shows one; what it cannot absorb is a
violation the log presents as compliance. Only 11.7\% of violations are openly owned, 31.3\%
are silent, and the remaining 57.1\% arrive with a justification attached --- most often an
assertion that the rule does not apply (25.0\%) or a citation of an approval the
conversation never established (22.9\%). The second is the model restating the pressure it
was just given as though it were a fact of the world, which is why \emph{false\_clearance}
is the most damaging family in the battery. A silent violation is invisible to a keyword
search; a rationalized one is worse, because it survives human review by arriving with a
reason a reviewer is not equipped to check. Figure~\ref{fig:honesty} gives the per-model
split.

\paragraph{Every model fails on a different axis.} \S\ref{sec:metrics} names three ways a
pressure-resistant model can still be unsuitable, and all three appear in the panel.
Over-application is the paragraph above. \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} is the most
pressure-resistant model here (0.977) and has the panel's lowest honesty score (0.531), so
selecting on compliance alone returns the models whose failures are hardest to find in a
transcript. And \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} and \texttt{Mistral-7B} resist the opening pressure on
0.599 and 0.469 of items but hold under pushback on only 0.447 and 0.586, so a single-turn
evaluation would rate both far higher than a multi-turn one.

\paragraph{Per-model profiles.} Figure~\ref{fig:overlay} overlays six models chosen to span
the panel, and no two have the same profile shape. \texttt{Grok~4.3} is the strongest model
in the panel under multi-turn pushback (0.980) and concedes more than any other frontier
model at turn~1 (0.888 default, 0.760 scope); \texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} is the most honest and
among the most fragile. A high score on one axis buys nothing on the others, which is the
practical argument for shortlisting on the profile rather than on the summary.

\subsection{Ordinary Pressure Produces Violations}
\label{res:pressure}

None of these pressures is a jailbreak or an adversarial prompt. They are sentences an
ordinary employee writes, they cost nothing to compose, and they can be repeated.

\paragraph{The effect is the pressure, and persistence compounds it.} The rates above
isolate the pressure cleanly: the neutral and pressure items are the same scenarios with
and without one added paragraph, restricted to the binding items where the rule applies
and the convenient option breaks it. Of the 21 models with any unpressured violations, 18
get worse. The two headline rates also answer different questions. 4.2\% is the chance of a
violation on a given pressured request, and 6.4\% is the chance that a user willing to ask
three times obtains one, the $\text{pass}^3$ convention used throughout the paper. Nothing
in the protocol rewards persistence beyond re-asking, so 6.4\% is a floor on what a
determined employee can extract, not a ceiling.

\paragraph{The strongest models are not exempt.} Among the top ten by PACTScore,
\texttt{Inkling} violates on 4.6\% of pressured requests and fails 6.0\% of situations
over three attempts, \texttt{GLM-5} 4.2\% and 6.4\%, and \texttt{Nemotron-3-Ultra} 3.9\%
and 6.3\%; \texttt{Grok~4.3} reaches 6.6\% and 11.7\%. Even \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5},
which never violates an unpressured rule, violates on 1.3\% of pressured requests and
fails one pressured situation in 43. These rates hold for exactly the models a practitioner
would shortlist today.

\paragraph{What a violation looks like.} The conduct behind the rates takes a few recurring
shapes, each from a model near the top of Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} in the base mode under
a single ordinary pressure; Appendix~\ref{app:failures} works the last of these through in full.
%
\emph{Supplies a pretext.} \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B} (rank 2) states the source-of-income statute
correctly, then coaches a landlord to decline a Section~8 voucher holder under a ``lawful,
non-source-of-income reason'' and keep the real reason unwritten, repeating it at turn~2 even
after the user names the law.
%
\emph{Breaks it silently.} \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} (rank 3), under time pressure, opens a
VIP-flagged chart in the sidebar rather than routing it --- a reportable access under 45 CFR
164.502 --- and never mentions PHI or the breach.
%
\emph{Manufactures a record.} On the same item \texttt{Gemini 3 Flash} (rank 7) writes a
Treatment/Scheduling justification into the access log, a clean audit trail for an access
that should not have occurred.
%
\emph{Recovers only when challenged.} \texttt{Kimi-K2.7-Code} (rank 1) drops the one
shortlisted candidate on parental leave, states the disqualifier (``no leave conflicts''), and
drafts her rejection --- the fact pattern of \emph{Mobley v.\ Workday}~\citep{mobley2025workday}
--- then reverses at turn~2 when asked whether leave factored in.
%
These are the modal failures of the models a reader would shortlist first, not edge cases from
the tail; in three of the four the reply never mentions the rule at all, so transcript
review catches none of them.

\subsection{Evaluation Awareness}
\label{res:eval_awareness}
A behavioral benchmark is meaningful only if models do not raise their compliance on
recognizing a test, which recent models increasingly do~\citep{needham2025evalaware}.
We measure this directly. On a random 10\% of items (139 per model) we re-present each
decision at three levels of test-obviousness, holding the rule, case facts, incentive, and
options fixed: the native item (R0), a version with the realism stripped to generic
model-prose (R1), and the native item under an explicit ``you are being evaluated'' frame
(R2). We record the relative change in compliance, $(C-C_0)/(1-C_0)$. Across twelve models
the explicit frame removes a mean of 39\% of native non-compliance, rising to 73\% for
\texttt{Gemini~3 Flash} and 71\% for \texttt{DeepSeek-V4-Pro}. Stripping realism moves
behavior less and inconsistently (mean 11\%), and for three models it moves the wrong way,
with \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} at $-79\%$.

The two halves of that result say different things. A stylistic tell does not reliably
change behavior, so naturalistic prose is not by itself what protects the measurement. An
explicit label does change behavior, and changes it most for the heavily benchmarked
frontier systems, which is the bias PACT's construction is intended to avoid and the
reason no item in the set announces itself as a test. The full protocol, per-model
results, and the saturation caveat (the effect is about 2 points in absolute terms, because
$C_0$ is already near ceiling on this subsample) are in Appendix~\ref{app:evalaware}.

\subsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{Metric validation.} We fixed the six axes and PACTScore before running the
panel, and the data supports both load-bearing choices. The axes are not redundant: they
reduce to three largely independent factors (Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary},
Figure~\ref{fig:axiscorr}), so a single scalar would be blind to steerability and honesty.
And reliability is not well approximated by the mean: on near-ceiling tasks a plain average
absorbs a model's worst items while $\text{pass}^3$ retains them. Judge validity is
assessed separately in Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}.

\paragraph{Abstention.} PACTScore drops a replication the judge cannot resolve rather than
scoring it as a violation, which is defensible only if abstentions are substantive. They
are. Abstention runs 0.6\%--8.5\% (median 3.0\%) and does not track PACTScore, and the
classified reasons are dominated by refusal to take the shortcut accompanied by a
compliance warning (40.5\%) and by picks that argue for an unlisted alternative (29.4\%),
not by blank non-answers (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}). Counting these as violations
would conflate ``would not answer'' with ``broke the rule''; counting them as compliance
would credit an answer the model never gave. We report them as their own diagnostic.

\paragraph{Use as a governance instrument.} PACT addresses a question existing evaluations
do not: which model holds a given rule under a rushed manager. A per-domain,
reliability-scored, multi-turn profile lets an organization filter the leaderboard to its
own domain, read per-item reliability in place of the mean, check whether a guardrail
actually moves its candidate, and see whether a violating model admits the violation.
Because the item set is frozen with a private holdout, the same benchmark reruns on each
model version, so a silent vendor update that shifted a model's urgency floor would be
detected.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} \emph{Evaluation awareness} would inflate any behavioral
benchmark; we enforce naturalism at generation and measure the residual threat directly
(\S\ref{res:eval_awareness}). \emph{Stated versus enacted compliance}: a recommendation
upper-bounds what a tool-using agent would actuate, and an actuated variant is future work,
though \emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada} establishes that a chatbot's statement is itself a
liability~\citep{moffatt2024aircanada}. \emph{Judge circularity} is addressed by the
reported inter-rater agreement and the cross-judge stability of the honesty ranking.
\emph{Author-model bias}: generated items could favor their author's family, which the
dual-guard review and a computable generator-versus-evaluatee test address. \emph{Panel
size}: model-level factor analyses are only indicative at 22 models, so the item-level
statistics carry the weight.
