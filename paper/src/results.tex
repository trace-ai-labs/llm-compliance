\section{Validating the Instrument}
\label{sec:validation}

A benchmark's numbers are only as trustworthy as the measuring instrument that
produced them, and the instrument here (a floor-sensitive rollup, a generated item
set, and an LLM-assisted judge) makes several assumptions that can fail silently.
We therefore report two things before any model leaderboard: the validation record
that shaped the metric design, and the reporting structure the frozen benchmark
produces.

\subsection{Metric Validation on a Precursor Study}
\label{sec:selfval}

The metric design was not chosen a priori. It was stress-tested on a
72{,}142-trial precursor study of the procurement anchor (twelve models under a
single embedded rule, varied by framing, enforcement, authority, peer signals, and
multi-turn pushback) and revised where the data contradicted an assumption.
Table~\ref{tab:selfval} summarizes the tests that changed or confirmed a design
decision; each is reproducible from the released re-analysis code.

Three findings justify the benchmark's most distinctive choices. \textbf{The floor
is not the mean.} A Kendall $\tau$ of 0.70 between the CVaR and mean leaderboards
means the floor-weighting genuinely reorders models, so publishing the mean as the
headline would rank models by exactly the quantity that hides the cliff a deployer
falls off. \textbf{The battery is not collapsible.} Cross-family transfer ranging
from 0.33 to 0.84 says the pressure families measure partially distinct properties;
an authority-robust model is not reliably urgency-robust, so pruning to one pressure
would discard information a governance user needs. \textbf{The paradox is real.} A
truthfully described weak penalty \emph{reducing} compliance, significant for 10 of
12 models, is the ``fine is a price'' effect~\citep{gneezy2000fine} reproduced in
machines, and it is directly actionable: for paradox-prone models, quantitative
penalty language should be removed from system prompts. These are the empirical
warrants for the cross-fitted-CVaR floor, the nine-family battery, and the
theory-grounded framing.

\subsection{What the Frozen Benchmark Reports}
\label{sec:reporting}

Applied to the frozen 1{,}872-item set across a 12-model panel, the pipeline
produces, per model: the six-axis profile with per-axis $\kappa$; the scalar
rollup (cross-fitted CVaR with a two-stage-bootstrap interval, its plain-mean gap,
the harmonic mean, and the mean win rate); the fragility-breadth count; the
inter-axis correlation matrix; the rule-feature regression across the 48 scenarios;
and the evaluation-awareness rate as a validity flag. Per-cell tables carry Wilson
intervals and clustered standard errors, and per-model contrasts carry
Benjamini--Hochberg-adjusted bootstrap $p$-values. Because the full design has been
frozen and pre-registered but the 12-model evaluation sweep is still in progress at
submission time, we report the instrument and its validation here and release the
leaderboard, per-trial outcomes, and all alternative aggregations with the artifact
rather than asserting model rankings we have not yet completed.

\subsection{Un-Gameability, Demonstrated}
\label{sec:gameability}

The four trivial agents are the benchmark's built-in falsification test, run on
every release without model calls. Each is designed to top a na\"ive metric and
fail the real one. \textsc{always-comply} maximizes raw compliance but scores 0.5 on
rule-scope discernment and breaches the needless-escalation ceiling;
\textsc{always-cheapest} floors pressure resistance; \textsc{always-escalate} is
caught by the discernment guard's over-escalation term; and \textsc{random} sits at
chance on every axis. The release invariant is explicit: if any trivial agent
out-ranks a real model on any axis, that axis is broken and is pulled from the
headline. This turns un-gameability from a claim into a per-release check, and it is
why the discernment axis is scored as balanced accuracy with a guard twin rather
than as raw compliance.
