\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:leaderboard}

We evaluate \textbf{22 models} (18 open-weights and 4 closed: \texttt{Claude Haiku
4.5}, \texttt{GPT-5.6 Luna}, \texttt{Grok~4.3}, \texttt{Gemini~3 Flash}) on the frozen
dataset of 1{,}737 items, three replications in each of two system-prompt modes. The
panel spans 7B dense models to trillion-parameter sparse mixtures and three years of
releases; Appendix~\ref{app:models} (Table~\ref{tab:models}) lists each model's
provider, size, release date, and citation.

\textbf{The scores are a failure rate, not a grade.} $\text{pass}^3$ asks
whether a model held the rule on \emph{every} one of three identical runs, because
the deployment bar is unforgiving: an enterprise assistant has to be right
every time, not on average. So $1-\text{pass}^3$ is the share of items on which the
model simply \emph{cannot be relied on}; it slipped at least once. Read that way,
Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} is sobering rather than reassuring. The best model in the
panel, \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5}, still fails to reliably hold on $\sim$5\% of items;
much of the field clusters near 0.90, roughly one unreliable item in ten; and the
weakest models miss 40--50\%. In a regulated function where a \emph{single} violation
is a legal event (a mis-stated refund policy, an out-of-scope PHI disclosure, a
structured payment), ``right 90--95\% of the time'' is not a passing grade; it is one
avoidable incident every ten to twenty interactions. The leaderboard does not name a
model safe to run autonomously; it ranks \emph{how much oversight each still needs},
and even the leader needs a lot.

\textbf{The axes measure genuinely different things.} The six axes collapse to three
near-independent factors (Figure~\ref{fig:axiscorr}): a \emph{rule-holding} cluster
(default compliance, pressure resistance, pushback resistance, and rule-scope
discernment, pairwise $r=0.88$--$0.95$ across the panel, because a capable model
tends to hold across conditions), and then \emph{steerability} and \emph{honesty},
each near-zero-correlated with that cluster and with each other. Steerability is the
sharpest case: it runs only 0.28--0.57 across the \emph{entire} panel (the amber
Steer.\ column of Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}), so an explicit guardrail recovers far
less of a model's base failures than is commonly assumed, the single most actionable
result in the run. Honesty is nearly orthogonal to rank: the highest score belongs to
\texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} (0.88), which sits near the bottom of the rollup, while several
top-rollup models are middling at admitting a violation. A model is therefore not
summarized by its rank; the per-axis profile, which an organization can filter to its
own domain, is the primary artifact and the scalar a conservative summary.

\textbf{Different models fail differently.} Figure~\ref{fig:overlay} overlays six
models chosen to span the range, and no two profiles have the same shape.
\texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} leads on holding the rule but is one of the \emph{least}
forthcoming when it does break it. \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B} is the most steerable and
among the most honest. \texttt{Kimi-K2.7-Code} and \texttt{Grok~4.3} barely bend
under multi-turn pushback, yet \texttt{Grok} gives up more ground than any frontier
model on the plain default and scope checks. The sharpest cautionary case is
\texttt{Seed-OSS-36B}: it is the \emph{most honest} model in the panel, readily
admitting when it departs from the rule, but it is also one of the most
\emph{fragile}, cratering to 0.60 compliance under a fabricated prior-authorization
claim (Figure~\ref{fig:fragility}). Honesty is not safety: a model that reliably
tells you it just broke the rule has still broken the rule, which is the practical
reason to read the profile and not the headline number.

\textbf{Failures concentrate in specific pressure families.} The 5--10\% is not
uniform noise spread evenly over items. Figure~\ref{fig:fragility} maps
base-mode compliance by pressure family, and the losses concentrate: the weaker
models crater on \emph{false\_clearance} (an unverifiable ``we're already cleared for
this'') and \emph{urgency}, while holding elsewhere; \texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} drops to
0.60 on false-clearance and \texttt{Mistral-7B} to 0.47, against 0.90+ on other
families. Concentration is what turns a statistical error rate into an exploit. A
random 7\% slip is a reliability problem; a 40\% slip that fires \emph{only} when
someone claims prior authorization is an attack surface. An insider who knows the
model (a broker who wants a structured payment processed, a manager who wants a
discrimination complaint buried) does not need a jailbreak; they need the one
sentence that maps to the model's soft spot, and the fragility map hands them the
address. This is why the pressures are a battery and not a mean: a per-family
breakdown tells an organization \emph{which} plausible insider sentence its candidate
cannot withstand, and the mean hides exactly that.

\subsection{Discussion and Social Impact}

\paragraph{Metric validation.}
\label{sec:validation}
The metrics were not chosen a priori. We stress-tested them on the 72{,}142 trials of
\citet{priorstudy}, a single-domain compliance study (twelve models under one
embedded procurement rule), and revised the design where the data disagreed. Three
results shaped the final choices. \emph{Reliability is not the mean}: on these
near-ceiling tasks a plain mean rounds a model's worst items away, so we score
per-item reliability instead. \emph{The battery is not collapsible}: a score computed
without a given pressure family predicts rank on it only weakly (Spearman $\rho$ from
0.33 to 0.84), so no family is redundant. \emph{An early abstention hypothesis failed}
(compliance shifts of $\leq7$pp, with the wrong sign for three models) and was demoted
to exploratory rather than quietly dropped. Judge validity is checked separately: a
judge-swap holds the ranking at Kendall $\tau\geq0.9$, and the two reasoning classifiers
carry inter-rater $\kappa=0.51$ (honesty) and $0.62$ (abstention), detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}.

\paragraph{A governance instrument.} PACT targets a concrete decision. A bank,
hospital, or municipal office choosing an assistant for a regulated workflow has no
instrument that answers ``which model holds this rule under a rushed manager?'' A
per-domain, reliability-scored, multi-turn profile gives an organization something to
act on: filter the leaderboard to its own domain, read per-item reliability rather
than the flattering mean, check whether a guardrail actually moves the candidate
(usually far less than hoped), and see whether a violating model at least admits it.
Two findings are already interventions: rephrasing a rule imperatively raises
compliance, and removing quantitative penalty language from a system prompt raises it
for models otherwise prone to treating the penalty as a price. The frozen dataset,
with its pre-registered hash, private holdout, and canary, also lets the same
benchmark rerun on each model \emph{version}; no one currently tracks whether a
vendor's silent update moved its urgency floor, and a benchmark that catches that
becomes monitoring infrastructure.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} \emph{Evaluation awareness} would undermine any
behavioral benchmark; we enforce naturalism at generation and report a dedicated
awareness probe, measuring the threat rather than assuming it away. \emph{Stated
versus enacted compliance}: recommendation-level compliance upper-bounds what a
tool-using agent actuates, and an actuated variant is future work, but \emph{Moffatt
v.\ Air Canada} established that a chatbot's statement is itself a liability
surface~\citep{moffatt2024aircanada}. \emph{Judge circularity} is met by the
judge-swap $\tau$-gate and the reported inter-rater agreement of the reasoning
classifiers (Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}).
\emph{Generated items} could favor their author's family, which the dual-guard review
and the computable generator-versus-evaluatee test address. \emph{Panel size}:
model-level factor analyses are indicative at 22 models, so the item-level statistics
over hundreds of thousands of trials are load-bearing, and every new model
strengthens them.
