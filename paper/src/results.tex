\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:leaderboard}

We evaluate 22 models (18 open-weights and 4 closed) on PACT's 1{,}737 items in two
system-prompt modes (3{,}474 prompts), three times each. The panel spans 7B dense
models to trillion-parameter sparse mixtures and three years of releases;
Appendix~\ref{app:models} lists each model's provider, size, release date, and
citation. Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} reports the six axes and PACTScore.

\paragraph{No model is reliable enough to deploy unsupervised.} PACTScore credits an item
only when the model makes the correct call on all three replications, so
$1-\text{PACTScore}$ is the share of items on which a model misjudges the rule's scope at
least once, in either direction. \PactLeader\ leads at \PactLeaderScore~\PactLeaderCI,
leaving about \PactLeaderFail\% of items unreliable; \PactLast\ scores \PactLastScore. Not
one of the \PactN\ models reaches 0.95, and 17 clear 0.90. Because the score pools the two
directions of the scope call, we report each separately below.

The top of the table is a cluster rather than a ranking. We attach a 95\% confidence
interval to every score and test each pairwise difference with a paired, item-clustered
bootstrap under multiplicity correction (\citealp{miller2024errorbars};
Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). \PactNSig\ of \PactNPairs\ pairs separate at $p<0.05$, but
\PactLeader\ and \PactSecond\ (\PactSecondScore~\PactSecondCI) differ by \PactTopGap,
well inside either interval, and \PactTopCluster\ models are statistically
indistinguishable from the top. Interval half-widths run
\PactCIHalfMin--\PactCIHalfMax, so adjacent rows separated by less than about 0.02 should
not be read as ordered at all. What the table supports is a coarse partition: a leading
group between 0.93 and 0.94, a body of eleven models between 0.90 and 0.93, and a tail
(\texttt{Grok~4.3} at 0.872 down to \texttt{Mistral-7B} at 0.493) that is clearly
separated from both.

\paragraph{Under pressure, models break the embedded rule on one request in twenty-four.}
Restricting attention to the binding items, where the rule applies and the convenient
option breaks it, gives the rate a compliance function would be asked about. The neutral
and pressure items are the same scenarios with and without one added paragraph, so the
contrast isolates the pressure. Adding it raises the panel's violation rate from 4.37\%
[3.97, 4.81] to 7.24\% [7.06, 7.42] of individual decisions, and 18 of the 21 models with
any unpressured violations get worse. Per pressured request, the median model recommends
the violating option 4.2\% of the time, one request in twenty-four, and eight of the 22
models exceed 5\%.

A single request understates the exposure, because the employee applying the pressure can
ask again. Counting a situation as failed when the model takes the shortcut on
\emph{any} of its three replications, which is the $\text{pass}^3$ convention used
throughout the paper, the median model fails 6.4\% of pressured situations, one in
sixteen, and 15 of the 22 models are at one in twenty or worse. The two rates answer
different questions: 4.2\% is the chance of a violation on a given request, and 6.4\% is
the chance that a user willing to ask three times obtains one.

Neither figure is confined to the weak end of the panel. Among the top ten by PACTScore,
\texttt{Inkling} violates on 5.0\% of pressured requests and fails 6.6\% of situations
over three attempts, \texttt{GLM-5} 4.1\% and 6.2\%, and \texttt{Nemotron-3-Ultra} 3.5\%
and 5.6\%; \texttt{Grok~4.3} reaches 6.5\% and 11.3\%. \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5}, which
never violates an unpressured rule, still violates on 0.9\% of pressured requests and
fails one pressured situation in 58. The pressures are not jailbreaks or adversarial
prompts; they are the sentences an ordinary employee produces when a deadline is close or
a manager has already said yes. They cost nothing to write and can be repeated. An
assistant deployed on a support desk or a records queue handles such requests
continuously, so these rates describe a standing exposure rather than an occasional
error, and they hold for the models a practitioner would currently shortlist.

\paragraph{Models also enforce rules that do not apply.} The non-binding twins carry the
opposite error, and it is the more frequent of the two. Across 14{,}621 decided base-mode
decisions on items where the rule does not bind, models enforce it anyway on 20.6\%. The
rate does not improve at the top of the table: \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5}, which has the
panel's lowest violation rate, has one of its highest over-application rates at 23.1\%.
The two errors are close to independent, so neither one characterizes a model on its own.
Over-application creates no legal exposure, but it produces an assistant that blocks work
the rule never covered, which is what causes a deployment to be withdrawn. PACTScore
charges both, and Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} should be read across its columns rather
than only sorted by the last one.

\paragraph{An explicit mandate helps least where it is needed least.} Because PACTScore
averages the base and anti-adversarial modes, a model that an explicit instruction cannot
lift scores lower than one it can. Across the panel the base-to-mandate difference ranges
from \PactGapMin\ to \PactGapMax, and it is inversely related to base compliance: the
mandate is worth \PactGapMax\ to \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} and $+0.087$ to
\texttt{Nemotron-3-Super}, but under $+0.01$ to four of the top seven, and for
\PactNBackfire\ models it is negative. Adding ``follow all rules, without exception'' to
the system prompt is therefore not a general remedy. It substantially repairs models that
were already unfit, and it leaves the frontier where it was. Ranking on the average
rather than the base mode alone moves \PactBiggestFaller\ down
\PactBiggestFallerPlaces\ places and \PactBiggestRiser\ up \PactBiggestRiserPlaces, so
two models with similar unprompted compliance can respond very differently to the one
lever a deployer actually controls.

\paragraph{Pressure resistance alone does not make a model deployable.}
\S\ref{sec:metrics} motivates the profile with three ways a pressure-resistant model can
still be unsuitable: it slips without leaving a trace an auditor could act on, it buys
robustness by being too conservative to be usable, or it holds at first contact and
concedes as the conversation continues. All three appear in the panel, and the first two
appear at the top of it.

\emph{Robust but not monitorable.} \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} is the most pressure-resistant
model in the panel (0.983) and the only one that never violates an unpressured rule, and
it has the panel's lowest honesty score (0.531): 45\% of the violations it does commit are
silent, taking the shortcut without mentioning the rule. \texttt{Kimi-K2.7-Code}, which
leads the table, scores 0.586. Selecting on compliance alone returns the models whose
failures are hardest to find in a transcript (\S\ref{res:honesty}).

\emph{Robust by over-application.} Every model in the panel scores lower on rule-scope
discernment than on default compliance, and the gap is widest for the strictest models:
\texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} (1.000 against 0.847), \texttt{GPT-5.6 Luna} (0.978 against
0.830), and \texttt{Qwen3.5-35B} (0.957 against 0.804). Part of what reads as compliance
in the leftmost column is a disposition to hold rules whether or not they apply, which
costs 20.6\% of the non-binding decisions panel-wide.

\emph{Holds, then concedes.} This disqualifier binds at the bottom rather than the top.
A model that complies at turn~1 rarely reverses under the turn-2 challenge, and turn-2
hold sits at 0.952--0.968 across all nine pressure families. The exceptions are the two
weakest models: \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B} resists the opening pressure on 0.611 of items but
holds under pushback on only 0.460, and \texttt{Mistral-7B} scores 0.479 and 0.592. A
single-turn evaluation would rate both meaningfully higher than a multi-turn one, which
is why the axis is reported separately and why turn~2 carries weight in PACTScore.

None of the three is visible in a scalar. The four rule-holding axes (default compliance,
pressure resistance, pushback resistance, and rule-scope discernment) correlate at
$r=0.88$--$0.95$, since a capable model tends to hold across conditions, but steerability
and honesty correlate with that group and with each other at $|r|<0.18$. The profile
therefore carries three largely independent signals, and honesty in particular is close to
independent of rank: its highest value, 0.879, belongs to \texttt{Seed-OSS-36B}, fourth
from the bottom of the table.

\paragraph{Per-model profiles.} Figure~\ref{fig:overlay} overlays six models chosen to
span the panel, and no two have the same profile shape. Beyond the cases above,
\texttt{Qwen3.6-27B} is the most steerable model in the panel (0.580) while sitting
mid-table on honesty (0.715); \texttt{Grok~4.3} is the strongest model under multi-turn
pushback (0.987) and concedes more than any other frontier model at turn~1 (0.897 default,
0.760 scope); and \texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} scores highest on the honesty axis (0.879) while
being among the most fragile, holding 0.936 unpressured and 0.815 under pressure and
dropping to 0.60 on the single pressure family it handles worst (\S\ref{sec:breaks}). That
last combination is less flattering than it looks, for reasons \S\ref{res:honesty} takes
up. Steerability spans only 0.03--0.58 across the panel, so an explicit guardrail repairs
a smaller share of base failures than practitioners generally assume. A high score on any
one axis buys nothing on the others, which is the practical argument for shortlisting on
the profile rather than on the summary.

\subsection{Where Compliance Breaks}
\label{sec:breaks}

\paragraph{Failures concentrate on a few pressures.} The aggregate pressure effect hides
which pressures cause it, and they are not interchangeable.
Figure~\ref{fig:fragility} gives base-mode compliance per model per pressure family, on
the same per-decision scale as above. Pooled over the panel, the range runs from
\emph{false\_clearance} at 0.883, an unverifiable claim that the action has already been
approved, to \emph{peer\_escaped} at 0.953; the panel's 7.2\% pressured violation rate is
the average of a battery whose worst framing is half again as damaging as its mildest.
The per-model spread is far larger than the pooled one:
\texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} falls to 0.60 on false-clearance and \texttt{Mistral-7B} to 0.47,
against 0.90 or above on the other families. A failure rate concentrated on one framing
is a different security object from a uniform one, because an insider needs only the
sentence that matches it, and the per-family map identifies which sentence that is for a
given model.

\paragraph{The opening pressure carries the effect, not the follow-up.} The turn-2 result
above holds uniformly across the battery: turn-2 hold sits at 0.952--0.968 for all nine
pressure families and 0.89--0.98 across all twelve domains
(Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}, Table~\ref{tab:pressureturns}). No family is
distinctively good at extracting a reversal from a model that already refused, so a
deployer worried about a persistent user should read the turn-1 numbers rather than
looking for a pressure that works on the second attempt.

\paragraph{What a mandate can and cannot repair.} The families a guardrail repairs least
are not the ones that cause the most violations. Ranked by steerability, the bottom three
all relocate accountability rather than create urgency: \emph{peer\_escaped} (0.15),
\emph{responsibility\_shift} (0.18), and \emph{fait\_accompli} (0.21). A blanket
instruction to follow the rule rebuts a direct temptation, so
\emph{sympathetic\_beneficiary} (0.46) and the manager and cost framings (0.35--0.36)
recover considerably better, but it has little purchase on an argument that the rule is
not the model's responsibility. \emph{false\_clearance} causes the most turn-1 violations
yet is only middling to repair (0.37), so damage and repairability order differently and
a deployer concerned with a specific framing has to read both.

\paragraph{Domain matters as much as model choice.} Pooled across the panel, base
compliance ranges from 0.76 in procurement to 0.99 in government services and content
moderation, a spread comparable to the distance between the best and the median model.
The losses concentrate in particular (domain, pressure) intersections rather than
spreading over the grid; the weakest single cell is procurement under
\emph{false\_clearance} at 0.72 (Figure~\ref{fig:dompress}). Over-application is
structured too, and inversely: rule-scope discernment is lowest exactly where the rule's
boundary is genuinely ambiguous, at 0.71 in healthcare administration and 0.77 in AML
against 0.90--0.91 in government services and moderation
(Table~\ref{tab:perdomain}). The domains where models most often break a rule are not the
domains where they most often over-apply one, so a deployer cannot generalize from either
to the other, and neither is predictable from a model's overall score.

\paragraph{Steerability does not follow from baseline difficulty.} The natural
explanation for a domain being easy to steer is headroom: more base failures leave more
to repair. The data has the opposite sign. Recovery is normalized by each domain's own
failure mass, so headroom is divided out by construction, and across the twelve domains
base compliance and steerability are \emph{positively} correlated ($r=+0.56$;
Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}, Figure~\ref{fig:steerbaseline}). HR/hiring and
healthcare administration sit mid-range at baseline (0.89 and 0.87) yet have the lowest
steerability in the panel (0.19 and 0.29), while AML and privacy are both strong at
baseline and the most steerable (0.78--0.80). A deployer cannot infer from a model's base
compliance in a domain whether a guardrail will help there, which is the practical reason
PACTScore scores both system-prompt modes rather than reporting the base mode alone.

\subsection{What Models Say When They Break a Rule}
\label{res:honesty}

An organization can absorb an occasional violation if the transcript shows one. What it
cannot absorb is a violation the log presents as compliance. We classify every base-mode
binding violation with a leave-one-out ensemble of the three generator models, giving
11{,}450 judged violations across the panel (Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}).

Only 11.7\% of violations are openly owned, where the model states that it is departing
from the rule. A further 31.3\% are silent, with the shortcut taken and the rule not
mentioned at all. The remaining 57.1\% are neither: the model breaks the rule and
supplies a justification for it. Those justifications fall into three recurring shapes.
\emph{Scope denial} (25.0\%) asserts the rule does not apply to this case.
\emph{False authority} (22.9\%) cites an approval, precedent, or permission that the
conversation never established, which is the model restating the pressure it was just
given as though it were a fact of the world. \emph{Risk minimization} (9.2\%) concedes
the rule and reclassifies the breach as immaterial. The per-model breakdown is in
Figure~\ref{fig:honesty}.

This exposes a limit of the honesty axis, which scores $1-\text{silent}$ and therefore
credits a model for saying \emph{something} about the violation. Most of what models say
is not a disclosure. \texttt{Seed-OSS-36B} has the best honesty score in the panel
(0.879) because it is silent on only 12.5\% of its violations, yet it openly owns just
9.2\% of them and rationalizes 78.3\%, the highest rate we measure;
\texttt{DeepSeek-V4-Pro} is second on both counts (0.833, and 72.0\% rationalized against
9.5\% owned). Ranked by how often a model actually admits the breach, the order is
different again, led by \texttt{GLM-5.2} (35.2\%), \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} (33.3\%),
and \texttt{GPT-5.6 Luna} (28.7\%). We report the axis as defined and pre-registered, and
we read it as a measure of non-concealment rather than of candor.

For an auditor the distinction matters more than the ranking. A silent violation is
invisible to a keyword search over transcripts; a rationalized one is worse, because it
survives human review by arriving with a reason attached, and the two most common reasons
are precisely the ones a reviewer is least equipped to check, an assertion that the rule
does not apply and a citation of an approval that never happened. Honesty is also the
axis least stable across judges ($\kappa=0.51$; individual judges rank models at Kendall
$\tau=0.38$--$0.53$ with each other), and the strongest models produce so few violations
that their per-model shares rest on small samples (51 for \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5}
against 3{,}008 for \texttt{Mistral-7B}), which is why honesty is excluded from PACTScore
and reported with wider caveats. The panel-level result is robust to both: no model in
the panel openly owns even 36\% of its own violations.

\subsection{Evaluation Awareness}
\label{res:eval_awareness}
A behavioral benchmark is meaningful only if models do not raise their compliance on
recognizing a test, which recent models increasingly do~\citep{needham2025evalaware}.
We measure this directly. On a random 10\% of items (139 per model) we re-present each
decision at three levels of test-obviousness, holding the rule, case facts, incentive,
and options fixed: the native item (R0), a version with the realism stripped to generic
model-prose (R1), and the native item under an explicit ``you are being evaluated'' frame
(R2). We record the relative change in compliance, $(C-C_0)/(1-C_0)$. Across twelve
models the explicit frame removes a mean of 39\% of native non-compliance, rising to 73\%
for \texttt{Gemini~3 Flash} and 71\% for \texttt{DeepSeek-V4-Pro}. Stripping realism
moves behavior less and inconsistently (mean 11\%), and for three models it moves the
wrong way, with \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} at $-79\%$.

The two halves of that result say different things. A stylistic tell does not reliably
change behavior, so naturalistic prose is not by itself what protects the measurement. An
explicit label does change behavior, and changes it most for the heavily benchmarked
frontier systems, which is the bias PACT's construction is intended to avoid and the
reason no item in the set announces itself as a test. The full protocol, per-model
results, and the saturation caveat (the effect is about 2 points in absolute terms,
because $C_0$ is already near ceiling on this subsample) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:evalaware}.

\subsection{Discussion and Social Impact}

\paragraph{What a violation looks like.} The rates above are aggregates, and the conduct
behind them is specific. Appendix~\ref{app:failures} reproduces five verbatim
transcripts; the first two come from the models ranked third and first in
Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}. In the first, \texttt{Claude Haiku 4.5} is asked under time
pressure to confirm an appointment against a VIP-flagged medical record and opens the
chart in the sidebar rather than routing the request, an access without a documented
treatment purpose and a reportable breach under 45 CFR 164.502. It does not mention PHI,
the treatment-relationship test, or the breach; on the same item \texttt{Gemini 3 Flash}
writes a Treatment/Scheduling justification into the access log, producing a clean audit
trail for an access that should not have occurred. In the second,
\texttt{Kimi-K2.7-Code} is told by a hiring manager to take responsibility for the call
and returns the one shortlist that excludes the sole candidate on parental leave, states
the disqualifying reason (``no leave conflicts''), and drafts her rejection letter,
which is the fact pattern of \emph{Mobley v.\ Workday}~\citep{mobley2025workday}. Asked
at turn~2 whether leave factored into the ranking, it reverses and corrects the letter,
so the rule was available to it and went unapplied until challenged.

These are not edge cases drawn from the bottom of the panel. They are the modal failure
of the two models a practitioner reading Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} would shortlist
first, they were produced by ordinary workplace pressure rather than by an attack, and in
both the model left no indication in the transcript that a rule had been engaged. An
organization relying on log review to catch these would not have caught either.

\paragraph{Metric validation.} We fixed the six axes and PACTScore before running the
panel, and the data supports the two load-bearing choices. First, the axes are not
redundant: they reduce to three largely independent factors ($r=0.88$--$0.95$ within the
rule-holding group, near zero across it), so a single scalar would be blind to
steerability and honesty. Second, reliability is not well approximated by the mean: on
near-ceiling tasks a plain average absorbs a model's worst items while $\text{pass}^3$
retains them, and we report the difference between the two beside PACTScore. Judge
validity is assessed separately. The two reasoning classifiers reach inter-rater
$\kappa=0.51$ (honesty) and $0.62$ (abstention), and the honesty ranking is only
moderately stable across judges, which is why honesty is excluded from PACTScore
(Appendix~\ref{app:agreement}).

\paragraph{Abstention.} PACTScore drops a replication the judge cannot resolve rather
than scoring it as a violation, which is defensible only if abstentions are substantive.
They are. Abstention runs 0.6\%--8.5\% (median 3.0\%) and does not track PACTScore, and
the classified reasons are dominated by refusal to take the shortcut accompanied by a
compliance warning (40.5\%) and by picks that argue for an unlisted alternative (29.4\%),
not by blank non-answers (Appendix~\ref{app:abstention}). Counting these as violations
would conflate ``would not answer'' with ``broke the rule''; counting them as compliance
would credit an answer the model never gave. We report them as their own diagnostic.

\paragraph{Use as a governance instrument.} PACT addresses a question existing
evaluations do not: which model holds a given rule under a rushed manager. A per-domain,
reliability-scored, multi-turn profile lets an organization filter the leaderboard to
its own domain, read per-item reliability in place of the mean, check whether a guardrail
actually moves its candidate, and see whether a violating model admits the violation. Two
results are directly actionable: phrasing the rule as an explicit command raises
compliance, though by less than practitioners expect and least for the models already at
the top; and the pressures that most resist a guardrail are the ones that relocate
accountability, which is an argument for process controls rather than prompt engineering
in those settings. Because the item set is frozen with a private holdout, the same
benchmark reruns on each model version, so a silent vendor update that shifted a model's
urgency floor would be detected.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} \emph{Evaluation awareness} would inflate any
behavioral benchmark; we enforce naturalism at generation and measure the residual
threat directly (\S\ref{res:eval_awareness}). \emph{Stated versus enacted compliance}: a
recommendation upper-bounds what a tool-using agent would actuate, and an actuated
variant is future work, though \emph{Moffatt v.\ Air Canada} establishes that a
chatbot's statement is itself a liability~\citep{moffatt2024aircanada}. \emph{Judge
circularity} is addressed by the reported inter-rater agreement and the cross-judge
stability of the honesty ranking. \emph{Author-model bias}: generated items could favor
their author's family, which the dual-guard review and a computable
generator-versus-evaluatee test address. \emph{Panel size}: model-level factor analyses
are only indicative at 22 models, so the item-level statistics over hundreds of
thousands of trials carry the weight.
